PART TWO. THE ONE THING I'D CHANGE. DON'T DEFAULT THE TOGGLE.

You said make the toggle required, and default it to something's broken so that the default routes to donor relations. I understand the instinct. The instinct is, fail safe. If somebody doesn't think about it, make sure the thing lands in front of a human rather than getting lost.

I'd argue against it, on two grounds.

The first is that required and defaulted are contradictory. A field with a default isn't required, it's pre answered. Required means the person has to make a choice. Defaulted means the choice has already been made for them and they can change it if they care. Those are opposites, and you can't get the benefit of both.

And in practice, defaults win. That's the most reliable finding in interface design. Most people accept the default, most of the time, especially when they're focused on something else, which on this form they always are, because they came here to type a sentence, not to file a report. So if something's broken is pre selected, a very large share of your submissions will be tagged something's broken regardless of what they actually say.

Which produces the second problem, and this one's worse. That toggle is your routing signal and it's also your data. It's the field that tells you what proportion of your inbound is friction versus ideas versus praise. That's a number you're going to want. It's the number that tells you whether the app is getting better.

If it's defaulted, that number is meaningless. You'll be looking at a list where seventy percent of everything says something's broken, and you won't be able to tell whether that's because the app is breaking or because that's where the toggle was sitting. You will have poisoned the one field you most need to trust, and you won't be able to un-poison it retroactively.

And the downstream effect is that donor relations gets flooded. Every I love it and every I have an idea that came in on the default gets routed to them as response owed work, and they have to open each one to discover it isn't. That's exactly the burden you built donor relations to prevent, arriving through a side door.

So here's the fix, and it's small. Make the toggle genuinely required. Nothing pre selected, all three chips sitting there neutral, and the send button stays disabled until they pick one. That's the same mechanic the send button already uses for the text box in what I built, so it's a one line change, and the interaction is already familiar to anyone who's used a form.

It costs the user one tap. And that one tap is the cheapest, highest quality routing signal you will ever get, because it's a person telling you directly which of three things they're doing.

Now, the safety concern behind your instinct. You wanted the default so nothing important slips past unseen. But look at your own architecture, because you already solved that. Every submission lands in the ClickUp list no matter what. Nothing gets lost, ever, regardless of the toggle. The list is the safety net. You don't need the default to be a second safety net, and using it as one costs you the integrity of the field.

If somebody miscategorizes a genuine problem as an idea, it's still in the list, you'll still see it, and retagging it is one click. That's a one click cost. The default costs you the whole dataset.

PART SEVEN. THE THINGS FROM THE SCRIPT YOU SKIPPED THAT ARE STILL WORTH HAVING.

Four things. I'll keep them short.

The first is the principle underneath your whole design, which you found on your own and which I'd initially gotten wrong. Response obligation and data destination are two separate decisions. I had told you that because help is response owed and feedback isn't, they should go to different places. That was a mistake. Your two rows, one list design is the thing that makes that concrete.

The second is why you were right that help requests are the most valuable feedback. Feature requests tell you what people think they want, filtered through their own theory of what software should do. Help requests tell you where the app actually failed a real person in a real moment. That's unfiltered friction. Every help request is a usability test you didn't have to run or pay for. And the aggregate is worth more than the individual ones, because ten people asking how to find the Rosary isn't ten tickets, it's one navigation problem. And each person who writes in is standing in for some number of people who hit the same wall and just closed the app without saying anything. The people who are delighted will tell you. The people who are stuck mostly just leave. Support volume is the only sample you'll ever get of the ones who are struggling.

The third is why the email address specifically was the thing to kill. Not because email is a bad way to talk to people, it's fine for that. Because email isn't a dataset. You can't filter it, tag it, count it, or export it, and you can't see the pattern across fifty messages unless one person reads all fifty and happens to notice. So routing help to an email address wasn't just a worse channel, it was a data leak, and it leaked precisely the highest value segment. Your instinct to be uneasy about it was right, and more right than the reason you gave for it.

The fourth is the quiet benefit of the design you landed on. Because both doors go to the same list, people picking the wrong door stops being expensive. Under a two destination design, a wrong door means something lands in the wrong system and gets lost or has to be forwarded. Under yours, a wrong door is a wrong tag, and retagging is one click. So you should not spend one more minute of design effort trying to stop people from misfiling. It costs you almost nothing now. That's a real structural win and it came from your design, not mine.
